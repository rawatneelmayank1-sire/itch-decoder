Decode

The feed sends changes, not state

If you have never worked with market data: this is the part that surprises people, and it is the reason the project exists.

ITCH is the raw feed Nasdaq broadcasts to anyone who wants to watch its market in full. It is not a quote service. There is no message that says here is the book. What arrives is an unbroken stream of length-prefixed binary records, each one a single event: an order was added, an order was cancelled, part of an order traded, an order was replaced. Every one is a delta.

So the price you would actually trade against — the best bid, the best offer, the depth behind them — exists nowhere on the wire. It only exists as the accumulated consequence of every message that came before, applied in exactly the order the exchange sent them. Reconstructing it is the price of admission for anything downstream: a spread study, a queue-position model, a fill simulator, a market-impact estimate.

And because the book is a running total, a single mishandled message is permanent. Miss one add and the order is invisible for the rest of the day. Apply a cancel out of order and the level is wrong from that instant onward. There is no correcting snapshot coming.

Which raises the obvious question: if the exchange never tells you the state, how do you know your state is right? The exchange does make one kind of falsifiable statement. When a trade prints, it names the order reference that traded. Nasdaq matches with strict price priority, so a displayed buy order trading at price P proves no better-priced displayed bid existed at that instant. Our book has to independently agree that P was its best bid, immediately before the message is applied.

Every execution in the file is checked that way. All — of them agree. That is a test against something the exchange said, not against another part of the same codebase — which is what makes it worth quoting.

The ladder above is one symbol’s reconstructed state at one instant. Scrub it and you are stepping through 5½ hours of pre-market, ending 30 seconds into the opening cross.

Scope

One venue’s displayed book, not the consolidated NBBO, over a pre-market window that ends 30 seconds into regular trading. The repository’s README.md, DESIGN.md and FAILURES.md state the limitations, the design arguments and the things that went wrong, in full.
